\begin{abstract}
ML-KEM spends most of its time in two primitives whose parallelism maps naturally
onto a SIMT warp but whose communication does not: a number-theoretic transform
(NTT) pairs lanes at power-of-two distances, whereas a Keccak round reduces
columns, gathers words, and combines row neighbors of a $5\times5$ state.
Software must synthesize this communication from shuffles, and a memory-coupled
accelerator moves the entire state through the cache on every call. In this
paper we present warp-collective instructions that keep both states in ordinary
lane registers of an open RISC-V GPU and expose the required communication as
fixed datapaths, while preserving the standard two-source, one-destination
operand contract. A pair-butterfly instruction returns both outputs of a
Cooley--Tukey or Gentleman--Sande butterfly from one modular product and shares
16 multipliers across 32 lanes. Keccak is offered at two granularities, three
per-stage instructions or one whole-round instruction, each producing one 32-bit
half of the next state on RV32. Implemented in the Vortex core and evaluated
with cycle-accurate XRT simulation and post-route Alveo~V80 implementation, the
collectives accelerate complete ML-KEM-768 by up to \RoundVsShuffleEight$\times$
over the same shuffle-based mapping and \RoundVsScalarOne$\times$ over scalar
software, for a \RoundLutThirtyTwoPct--\StageLutThirtyTwoPct\% LUT increase over
an NTT-equipped core at 250\,MHz. Whole-round fusion triples permutation
throughput yet improves complete requests by only \FusionAppEightPct\%, and the
memory-coupled engine overtakes register collectives at eight concurrent
requests. Collective granularity must therefore be chosen at application scope.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Post-quantum key establishment is now mandatory in TLS and VPN deployments,
and ML-KEM~\cite{fips203} is the standardized mechanism. A server terminating
many connections sees ML-KEM as a throughput workload, which makes a GPU an
attractive host. Each ML-KEM-768 request executes 140 Keccak-f[1600]
permutations~\cite{fips202} and 24 forward or inverse NTTs; on a scalar RISC-V
core, Keccak alone accounts for about \ScalarKeccakSharePct\% of a request and
the transforms for another \ScalarNttSharePct\%.

Both primitives contain enough independent work to occupy a 32-lane warp, but
they communicate across the warp in different ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:comm}). An NTT
butterfly exchanges one coefficient with a partner lane at distance
$16,8,4,2$ and applies a modular product; a Keccak round reduces five-word
columns, permutes and rotates all 25 words, and combines three row neighbors.
A SIMT core supports only lane-local arithmetic and generic shuffles, so a
cooperative software mapping pays for every pattern with shuffle and select
instructions: on our platform a 25-lane shuffle mapping still spends
\MicroPermShuffle{} cycles per permutation, and in a transform whose products
already use a modular-multiply instruction, shuffle communication accounts for
about two thirds of the cycles. The usual alternative, a
memory-coupled Keccak engine reached through a pointer, removes the
arithmetic but transfers the 200-byte state through the load/store unit on
each invocation and leaves absorb and squeeze on a single lane.

Register-coupled cryptographic extensions are well established for scalar
RISC-V cores~\cite{risqv,nannipieri}, a 25-thread Keccak layout has been used
in GPU software~\cite{mlcube}, and a warp-collective Keccak round has been
described in a patent application~\cite{collectivepatent}. What is missing is
an open implementation that fits such collectives into the operand and
writeback constraints of a real SIMT pipeline, and a controlled measurement of
how large a collective should be. A larger instruction removes issue slots and
shuffles, but it also adds pipeline storage, and its benefit is bounded by the
work that remains outside the primitive. Concurrency changes the answer again,
because a memory-coupled engine overlaps with other warps while a register
collective occupies the whole warp.

In this paper we present warp-collective NTT and Keccak instructions for the
open Vortex RISC-V GPU~\cite{vortex}. The key idea is to keep each primitive's
state in ordinary general-purpose registers, distributed across lanes in the
layout that its communication graph prefers, and to implement the cross-lane
edges of that graph as fixed wiring inside an execution unit that still reads
two source vectors and writes one destination vector. We make three
contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We define a register interface that fits multi-input, multi-output
cryptographic operations into standard SIMT operands: a pair butterfly whose
pair-low lane supplies the twiddle and whose two outputs return through the two
lanes' own destinations, and 25-lane Keccak stage and round instructions with
explicit low/high output halves on RV32. No instruction carries hidden state,
memory side effects, or a second destination.
\item We build datapaths matched to each communication structure: a
statically routed NTT unit that shares 16 multipliers across 32 lanes and
saves \HalfBankLutSavePct\% of core LUTs and \HalfBankDspSave{} DSPs at a
cycle cost below \HalfBankMaxCyclePct\%, and two-stage and four-stage Keccak
pipelines that add \StageLutThirtyTwoPct\% and \RoundLutThirtyTwoPct\% LUTs to
a routed 250\,MHz core.
\item We evaluate complete ML-KEM-768 rather than isolated primitives.
Register collectives speed up whole requests by up to
\RoundVsShuffleEight$\times$ over the same shuffle mapping and
\RoundVsScalarOne$\times$ over scalar software. Fusing a round into one
instruction triples permutation throughput but adds only
\FusionAppEightPct\% at request scope, and a pointer engine wins by
\PointerVsRoundEight$\times$ at eight concurrent requests despite losing at
one. The profile that explains both results is measured directly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\subsection{ML-KEM on a SIMT warp}
ML-KEM-768 works in $\mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^{256}+1)$ with $q=3329$. Its NTT
has seven levels with butterfly distances $128,64,\ldots,2$; each butterfly
reads a pair $(a,b)$ and a twiddle $\zeta$ and writes
$(a+t,\,a-t)$ with $t=\Mont(b\zeta)$. Hashing, sampling, and key derivation
use SHAKE and SHA-3, whose Keccak-f[1600] permutation applies 24 rounds of
$\theta$, $\rho\pi$, $\chi$, and $\iota$ to a $5\times5$ array of 64-bit
words. One request therefore alternates between polynomial arithmetic on 256
coefficients and permutations of a 1600-bit state, with sampling, encoding,
and control in between.

Vortex is an open SIMT GPU whose warps have 32 threads and whose execution
units are 32 lanes wide. Every ALU-class instruction reads up to three
source vectors from the register file, executes in one of several processing
elements, and commits one result vector, one word per lane. The ISA offers a
lane shuffle for cross-lane data movement and can be extended with custom
opcodes. Our final core has eight resident warps, 16\,KiB instruction and data
caches, 16\,KiB local memory, and no L2; the floating-point units are removed
so that every reported area is an integer core.

\subsection{Communication, not arithmetic, is the cost}
Fig.~\ref{fig:comm} shows the two communication graphs. Distributing a
polynomial as $R_k[l]=a[l+32k]$ makes the first three levels lane-local: the
partner of $R_k$ is $R_{k\,\mathrm{xor}\,4}$, $R_{k\,\mathrm{xor}\,2}$, or
$R_{k\,\mathrm{xor}\,1}$ in the same lane. The remaining four levels pair lane
$l$ with lane $l\,\mathrm{xor}\,d$ for $d=16,8,4,2$; there is no distance-one
level in ML-KEM. Keccak has no such split. With word $A[x,y]$ in lane $x+5y$,
$\theta$ needs the XOR of five lanes in a column, $\rho\pi$ moves every word to
a fixed different lane and rotates it, and $\chi$ needs the two right-hand
neighbors in the same row.

Software cooperative mappings can express all of these patterns with shuffles,
and they already beat scalar code (Table~\ref{tab:micro}): spreading one state
over 25 lanes completes a permutation \MicroShuffleVsPqrv$\times$ faster than
optimized RV32 assembly on one lane~\cite{pqrv}, and a five-lane column
mapping that exchanges words through local memory lies in between. But each
shuffled Keccak round still needs dozens of instructions per lane, and a
shuffled butterfly level needs a shuffle, two selects, and a product for every
coefficient. Fixed wiring can implement the same edges in one instruction,
provided that the instruction's inputs and outputs still fit the register file
interface; the last two rows of Table~\ref{tab:micro} are the result.

\subsection{Related work}
Register-coupled accelerators for scalar RISC-V cores implement NTT butterflies
and whole Keccak rounds inside the pipeline~\cite{risqv} or add modular
multiply and butterfly instructions~\cite{nannipieri}; they show the value of
register residence but have a single lane and no cross-lane problem. ML-Cube
maps Keccak to 25 GPU threads with shuffles~\cite{mlcube}, and an NVIDIA
patent application describes a warp-collective 64-bit Keccak
round~\cite{collectivepatent}; neither reports an implementation cost or a
granularity comparison. Near-memory NTT designs choose their operation size
from where butterfly partners reside~\cite{nttpim,bpntt}, the same principle
we apply to a register file. CHAM and ZK-Flex show that primitive-level
speedups in lattice and zero-knowledge accelerators shrink at application
scope~\cite{cham,zkflex}, and PacQ and SynGPU show how a SIMT datapath can be
extended without changing the general core~\cite{pacq,syngpu}. In contrast to
all of these, we implement both a lane-pair collective and a 25-lane
collective in one open GPU, keep the one-destination contract, and measure
granularity, concurrency, and area together on complete ML-KEM.

\section{Warp-Collective Instruction Design}
\subsection{Overview and contract}
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} places the new units in the Vortex pipeline. Three
processing elements sit beside the integer ALU and multiplier: an NTT unit,
a Keccak stage unit, and a Keccak round unit. Each is reached by the ordinary
execute interface, which delivers the warp's source vectors and thread mask,
and returns through the ordinary result interface into ALU arbitration and
commit. A memory-coupled pointer engine, attached to the load/store path, is
retained as the baseline for a coarser execution boundary.

All collectives obey one contract. An instruction reads two source vectors and
writes one destination vector, one word per lane. It has no pointer operand, no
memory ordering effect, and no state that survives between instructions; a
multi-instruction operation such as an RV32 round recomputes from identical
operands rather than retaining a partial result, so warps may interleave at
any instruction boundary and no context needs saving on a warp switch. Keccak collectives require all
32 lanes of the warp to be active, including the seven lanes that hold no
state and return zero; NTT collectives require both lanes of every enabled pair
to be active. These rules let the units use fixed wiring instead of a
permutation network and keep dependence tracking, backpressure, and writeback
arbitration unchanged.

\subsection{NTT: pair butterflies with one destination per lane}
A butterfly has three inputs and two outputs, so it does not fit a
two-source, one-destination instruction if each lane owns one coefficient.
The pair collective resolves this by letting the two lanes of a pair
cooperate on one product (Fig.~\ref{fig:pair}a). For a distance $d=2^s$,
lane $p$ with bit $s$ clear is the pair-low lane and $h=p\,\mathrm{xor}\,d$
is the pair-high lane. The first source vector supplies $a$ at lane $p$ and
$b$ at lane $h$; the second source vector supplies the twiddle at lane $p$, and
its value at lane $h$ is ignored by definition. The Cooley--Tukey (CT) form
computes
\begin{equation}
t=\Mont(b\zeta),\qquad r_p=a+t,\qquad r_h=a-t,
\end{equation}
and the Gentleman--Sande (GS) inverse form computes
\begin{equation}
r_p=\Barrett(a+b),\qquad r_h=\Mont\bigl((b-a)\zeta\bigr),
\end{equation}
with the library's signed 16-bit wrap-around semantics. Each lane writes its
own destination, so the pair's two outputs use two ordinary writebacks and no
second destination register. The instruction encodes $s\in\{0,\ldots,4\}$;
ML-KEM issues eight instructions per cross-lane level and 32 per transform
direction, and every twiddle is a per-lane operand rather than a table lookup
inside the unit. A separate lane-local instruction, \code{NTTMUL}, performs
one Montgomery product and serves the three in-lane levels, the inverse
scaling, and the base multiplication of polynomial arithmetic.

\subsection{Keccak: stage and round collectives}
Lane $l=x+5y$ holds word $A[x,y]$ as two 32-bit registers on RV32 or one
register on RV64. With coordinates modulo five, one round computes
\begin{align}
C[x]&=\textstyle\bigoplus_{y=0}^{4}A[x,y],\\
T[x,y]&=A[x,y]\oplus C[x{-}1]\oplus\Rot(C[x{+}1],1),\\
B[y,2x{+}3y]&=\Rot\bigl(T[x,y],\rho[x,y]\bigr),\\
U[x,y]&=B[x,y]\oplus\bigl(\neg B[x{+}1,y]\wedge B[x{+}2,y]\bigr),
\end{align}
followed by XOR of the round constant into lane zero. The \emph{stage}
design exposes three instructions, $\theta$, $\rho\pi$, and $\chi\iota$.
On RV32 each exists in a low and a high form, \code{.L} and \code{.H},
that read the same old state vectors and return the selected half of the new
word (Fig.~\ref{fig:keccak}a). Both halves must be computed from the full
64-bit words: the one-bit rotation in $\theta$ and the $\rho$ rotations cross
the half-word boundary, so two independent 32-bit operations would be wrong.
$\chi$ operates on one half at a time and takes a warp-uniform round index as
its second source. Six issues implement one round, and the programmer must not
overwrite an old half before the instruction that reads it has issued, which
is an ordinary register dependence.

The \emph{round} design fuses column parity, $\theta$, $\rho\pi$, and
$\chi\iota$ into one instruction with a literal round index
(Fig.~\ref{fig:keccak}b). On RV32, \code{KROUND.L} and \code{KROUND.H} each
read the complete old state and compute their half of the complete new state;
the pair recomputes from identical operands instead of keeping the second
half in hidden storage, so different warps may interleave freely. A
permutation is 48 RV32 issues instead of 144. RV64 changes only the word
interface: every stage returns a whole word and one \code{KROUND} issue
completes a round. Table~\ref{tab:isa} summarizes the collectives.

\subsection{Programming the complete request}
The ML-KEM implementation is mlkem-native~\cite{mlkemnative} compiled for
Vortex. Lane zero runs the scalar control flow. Wrappers around FIPS-202, the
transforms, and cooperative polynomial arithmetic activate the full warp,
broadcast the leader's arguments, execute the collective kernel, and restore
the leader mask. A request-level allocation arena replaces per-lane copies of
the workspace. The register-resident sponge keeps its state in lanes across
absorb, permutation, and squeeze of one call; the incremental SHAKE context
is saved to memory between API calls. The NTT kernel loads eight
coefficients per lane, runs all seven levels in registers, and stores them
back, so the transform makes no intermediate memory access.

The contract imposes three rules on kernel code. First, a Keccak collective
must be reached by all 32 lanes at the same instruction; loads and stores may
be guarded by \code{lane<25}, but the warp must reconverge before the
collective, and guarding the collective itself is a contract violation that
simulation reports. Second, the round operand of $\chi\iota$ must be uniform
across lanes and lie in $0,\ldots,23$; the round collective encodes the index
and needs no operand. Third, on RV32 the two halves of a stage must be issued
before either destination overwrites an old half, which the compiler satisfies
with one temporary. The permutation loop uses five general-purpose registers
and contains no load, store, call, or stack frame; the expanded round version
is 48 instructions with no branch.

\section{Implementation}
\subsection{Shared multiplier bank}
A 32-lane butterfly instruction needs only 16 products, so the NTT unit
instantiates 16 signed $16\times16$ multipliers (Fig.~\ref{fig:pair}b). Pair
operands are routed to bank element $i$ by static wiring for each of the five
encoded distances, which avoids a general permutation network. CT enters the
bank once. GS enters twice: the first beat forms $(b-a)\zeta$ and the second
forms the Barrett quotient product $(a+b)\cdot20159$, with the first result
held in a register until both are complete. Lane-local \code{NTTMUL} also
takes two beats, one per 16-lane half. With a registered reduction stage and a
registered result stage, CT has a latency of six cycles and an initiation
interval of one; GS and \code{NTTMUL} have seven and two. Multi-beat
operations stay adjacent under backpressure and results retain their lane
positions and warp metadata.

\subsection{Keccak pipelines}
The stage unit computes column parity, the $\rho\pi$ gather, or the $\chi$
row logic in its first stage and applies the $\theta$ correction or the round
constant in its second; two pipeline registers give an initiation interval of
one. The round unit has four registered stages, parity, $\theta$, $\rho\pi$,
and $\chi\iota$, also with initiation interval one; its stages hold complete
64-bit words, which is where its extra flip-flops come from. Padding lanes
25--31 are wired to zero. Simulation assertions check the full-mask,
uniform-round, and round-range rules, and instruction-level contract tests
exercise them with different twiddles per lane and partial masks.

\subsection{Verification and physical flow}
Unit tests check the butterfly against the reference for all five distances,
334,080 outputs in total, and the Keccak units against FIPS-202 for all 24
rounds, 10,200 word comparisons. Complete ML-KEM-768 requests reproduce the
FIPS 203 known-answer test byte for byte for every backend. Cycle results
come from XRT simulation of the accelerator function unit and processor RTL;
a functional timing model (SimX) reproduces the retired instruction counts
exactly and its whole-launch cycles agree within \ModelGapPct\% on the
matched controls. Physical results use Vivado~2025.1 for the Alveo~V80
(xcv80) with a 250\,MHz constraint, post-route physical optimization, and an
independent full-core run per configuration.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Methodology}
All performance runs use one RV32IM Vortex core with eight warps of 32
threads, the cache configuration of Sec.~II, and a single runtime that
enables the NTT unit, both Keccak units, and the pointer engine. Only the
Keccak backend of the software changes between arms; the NTT and polynomial
arithmetic collectives are fixed. We compare six backends: scalar reference
C, PQRV assembly~\cite{pqrv}, the 25-lane shuffle mapping (\emph{Shuffle}),
the stage collectives (\emph{Stage}), the round collective (\emph{Round}),
and the pointer engine (\emph{Pointer}). Each request performs key generation,
encapsulation, and decapsulation with the FIPS~203 known-answer inputs. We
report whole-launch device cycles for one request ($M{=}1$), which measures
latency, and for eight concurrent requests ($M{=}8$), which measures batch
completion time; overlapping per-request intervals are never summed.

\subsection{Complete ML-KEM-768}
Fig.~\ref{fig:kem} gives the headline result. The cooperative shuffle mapping
alone improves on PQRV assembly by \ShuffleVsPqrvOne$\times$ at $M{=}1$, so
scalar software is not a fair denominator for an instruction extension. Against
the identical shuffle mapping, the stage collectives improve complete requests
by \StageVsShuffleOne$\times$/\StageVsShuffleEight$\times$ and the round
collective by \RoundVsShuffleOne$\times$/\RoundVsShuffleEight$\times$ at
$M{=}1$/$M{=}8$. Over scalar C the round backend reaches
\RoundVsScalarOne$\times$ and \RoundVsScalarEight$\times$, and over PQRV
\RoundVsPqrvOne$\times$ and \RoundVsPqrvEight$\times$; these larger ratios
include the mapping change.

The best backend depends on concurrency. At one request the round collective
beats the pointer engine by \RoundVsPointerOne$\times$; at eight requests the
pointer engine finishes the batch \PointerVsRoundEight$\times$ faster than
Round and \PointerVsStageEight$\times$ faster than Stage. The batch gain from
one to eight requests is \BatchPointer$\times$ for Pointer and
\BatchScalar$\times$ for scalar C but only \BatchShuffle--\BatchStage$\times$
for the three 25-lane mappings, whose retired instructions per cycle at $M{=}8$
are \IpcStageEight{} against \IpcPointerEight{} for Pointer. All three
cooperative mappings issue lane-parallel sponge code that occupies the whole
32-wide datapath and load/store unit, whereas the pointer backend's
permutations execute in a separate unit and overlap with other warps. The
measurements establish the crossover; attributing it to one scheduler or
cache mechanism would need counters we do not have.

Instruction counts rule out the simplest explanation. At $M{=}8$ the round
backend retires \InstrRoundEight{} million instructions and the pointer
backend \InstrPointerEight{} million, so the pointer backend executes more
instructions and still finishes first. Its advantage is overlap, not work:
while one warp waits for the engine, the other seven issue scalar sponge and
polynomial code. A register collective has no such overlap; eight 25-lane
sponges contend for one 32-wide datapath, one load/store unit, and a 16\,KiB
data cache. The shuffle mapping, which uses no collective hardware, shows the
same small batch gain, so the effect belongs to the lane-parallel software
mapping rather than to the new units.

\subsection{Granularity: primitive gains versus request gains}
Round fusion cuts the RV32 issues per permutation from 144 to 48, but the
round kernel is also fully unrolled because the round index is a literal.
To isolate fusion we expand the stage kernel to the same 24-round straight-line
code and time 64 dependent permutations per warp. Fig.~\ref{fig:fusion} shows
the outcome. At primitive scope, Round completes a permutation in
\PermRoundOne{} cycles against \PermStageExpOne{} for expanded Stage with one
active warp, and \PermRoundEight{} against \PermStageExpEight{} per completed
permutation with eight warps, a \FusionPrimOne$\times$ and
\FusionPrimEight$\times$ gain. At request scope, the same two kernels in
complete ML-KEM differ by \FusionAppOne$\times$ at $M{=}1$ and
\FusionAppEight$\times$ at $M{=}8$.

\centering\input{figures/fusion.tex}

collectives. The primitive gain (64 dependent permutations per warp) does not

Fig.~\ref{fig:profile} explains the gap with directly measured, mutually
exclusive intervals of a single instrumented request. With stage collectives,
permutations take \PermStagePct\% of the request; with the round collective,
\PermRoundPct\%; with the pointer engine, \PermPointerPct\%. Absorb and
squeeze, which exclude nested permutations, take \SpongeStagePct\% for Stage
but \SpongePointerPct\% for Pointer, because the pointer backend runs them on
one lane and moves the state through memory around every permutation. The
transforms take about \NttStagePct\% and cooperative polynomial arithmetic
\PolyStagePct\%. The remainder, sampling, encoding, comparison, control, and
wrapper overhead, is the majority of the request for every backend. Amdahl's
law with $f=\PermStagePct\%$ and $s=\FusionPrimOne$ predicts a
$1/(1-f+f/s)\approx1.03\times$ gain from fusion, matching the measurement.
Instrumentation adds \ProbeMinPct--\ProbeMaxPct\% to these runs, so the
headline speedups use the uninstrumented launches.

\subsection{NTT communication and multiplier sharing}
The NTT study holds the modular multiplier fixed and changes only how the
four cross-lane levels communicate (Fig.~\ref{fig:ntt}). Replacing the
scalar product with \code{NTTMUL} speeds a forward transform up by
\NttMulVsCFwdOne$\times$; replacing shuffles with pair butterflies adds
another \NttPairVsShuffleFwdOne$\times$ (\NttPairVsShuffleInvOne$\times$ for
the inverse), so the communication instruction is worth more than the
arithmetic instruction. A stronger software control that performs the
cross-lane levels through a shared-memory transpose is still
\NttPairVsSmemFwdOne$\times$ slower at $M{=}1$ and \NttPairVsSmemFwdEight$\times$
at $M{=}8$. At request scope, pair butterflies reduce complete ML-KEM cycles by
\NttKemPairVsShuffleOnePct\%/\NttKemPairVsShuffleEightPct\% over the shuffle
version and \NttKemPairVsSmemOnePct\%/\NttKemPairVsSmemEightPct\% over the
shared-memory version at $M{=}1$/$M{=}8$; the transforms are a small share of
a request (Fig.~\ref{fig:profile}), so these are the expected magnitudes.

Sharing the multiplier bank is free at this scope. Against a matched
full-core implementation with one multiplier per lane, the 16-multiplier
unit removes \HalfBankLutSave{} LUTs (\HalfBankLutSavePct\%), \HalfBankFfSave{}

complete-KEM cycles by at most \HalfBankMaxCyclePct\%. The final NTT hierarchy
occupies \NttHierLut{} LUTs, \NttHierFf{} flip-flops, and \NttHierDsp{} DSPs.

\subsection{Choosing a design point}
Table~\ref{tab:points} collects the three hardware backends on the axes that
matter to a designer. The stage collectives add the fewest flip-flops and
expose three individually testable operations; they suit a register-limited
core. The round collective costs \RoundFfDeltaThirtyTwo{} flip-flops against
\StageFfDeltaThirtyTwo{} but fewer LUTs, gives the lowest single-request
latency, and provides about \RoundPerLutVsStage$\times$ the permutation
throughput per added LUT; it suits a latency-bound client or a hashing-heavy
workload. The pointer engine adds several times the logic of either
collective and loses at one request, but its permutations overlap with other
warps and it wins the eight-request batch by \PointerVsRoundEight$\times$.
None of this is visible from the permutation microbenchmark
(Table~\ref{tab:micro}), where the round collective leads shuffles by
\MicroRoundVsShuffle$\times$.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}
The evidence covers ML-KEM-768 at fixed known-answer inputs, two request
counts, and one core geometry; rejection sampling makes other inputs shift
absolute cycles for every backend alike. One state per warp is a fixed occupancy point, not an optimum;
scalar and five-lane software can pack more independent states into a warp
and would gain more on a hashing workload with many independent streams. All
cycle results are XRT simulation of the RTL rather than board execution, and
each physical row is one implementation seed. We make no energy claim, since
no activity-based power measurement exists, and no side-channel claim.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented warp-collective NTT and Keccak instructions that keep
cryptographic state in the ordinary registers of a RISC-V SIMT GPU and
implement each primitive's cross-lane communication as fixed wiring behind a
standard two-source, one-destination interface. A pair butterfly with 16
shared multipliers and stage or round Keccak collectives add about one
percent of LUTs to a routed 250\,MHz core and speed up complete ML-KEM-768 by
up to \RoundVsShuffleEight$\times$ over the same cooperative software mapping
and \RoundVsScalarOne$\times$ over scalar code. At request scope a threefold
permutation gain from round fusion becomes \FusionAppEightPct\%, and a
memory-coupled engine overtakes the register collectives at eight concurrent
requests. State placement, granularity, and concurrency must be chosen
together at the scope of the complete request.
