\title{Communication-Matched Warp Collectives for\\
ML-KEM and ML-DSA on a RISC-V SIMT GPU}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
ML-KEM and ML-DSA share Keccak and number-theoretic transforms (NTTs), yet
their useful parallelism has incompatible communication: Keccak repeatedly
reduces and permutes a 25-word state, while an NTT exchanges coefficient pairs
at power-of-two distances. We present communication-matched warp collectives
that keep both states in ordinary lane registers of an RV32 RISC-V GPU. The
instructions are stateless, use standard source vectors and single-destination
writeback, and implement fixed Keccak neighborhoods or paired NTT butterflies.
A serialized modular unit shares its routing and multiplier bank across the
16-bit ML-KEM and 32-bit ML-DSA datapaths. A 200-MHz V80 evaluation covers all
six standardized parameter sets against a matched, optimized cooperative
software mapping. Stage, shared NTT, and arithmetic reuse accelerate complete
single-request ML-KEM by \KemParameterMinOne{}--\KemParameterMaxOne{}$\times$
and ML-DSA by \DsaParameterMinOne{}--\DsaParameterMaxOne{}$\times$;
eight-request batches gain \ParameterMinEight{}--\ParameterMaxEight{}$\times$.
A separate five-point bank sweep selects two multipliers: it removes
\BankTwoNttDspSaved{}\% of NTT-hierarchy DSPs at a
\BankTwoNttPenalty{}\% standalone-cycle and at most
\BankTwoEndToEndMax{}\% complete-request penalty. The results show that GPU
cryptographic hardware must be sized at request scope, not by primitive peak
throughput.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
ML-KEM and ML-DSA standardize lattice-based key establishment and digital
signatures~\cite{fips203,fips204}. Their deployments must process independent
requests while retaining the exact rejection sampling, encoding, and control
of the standards. A programmable SIMT processor is attractive because a warp
can cooperate within one primitive and different warps can execute independent
requests. The challenge is data movement inside the warp.

The two shared primitives expose different graphs. An NTT level contains
128 independent butterflies, but its partner distance changes at every level.
Keccak-f[1600] instead applies column reductions, a fixed word permutation,
and row-neighbor logic to 25 tightly coupled 64-bit words. Generic lane
shuffles can express both graphs, but software pays an instruction and
dependence cost for every edge. A pointer accelerator removes more instructions
by moving a complete state to private storage, at the cost of transfers,
memory-system integration, and a new scheduling boundary.

Prior optimized RISC-V software and scalar extensions accelerate modular
butterflies and Keccak rounds~\cite{pqrv,risqv,nannipieri}; GPU software
distributes Keccak over 25 threads~\cite{mlcube}; and a patent describes a warp-level Keccak
round~\cite{collectivepatent}. These results do not answer three questions for
an open SIMT core: how a collective with multiple logical outputs fits
single-destination writeback, how ML-KEM and ML-DSA share one NTT datapath,
and whether primitive throughput predicts complete-request performance.

We answer these questions in Vortex~\cite{vortex} with a common rule: place a
primitive in lane registers, then implement only the fixed edges of its
communication graph (Fig.~\ref{fig:thesis}). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A stateless warp-collective interface for Keccak stages and XOR-paired
NTT butterflies. Each lane receives one result through normal dependency,
backpressure, and writeback machinery.
\item A shared K/D NTT unit whose pair routing and configurable multiplier bank
support seven-layer 16-bit ML-KEM and eight-layer 32-bit ML-DSA. A five-point
RTL and post-route sweep selects two multipliers.
\item A six-parameter board evaluation with matched software controls, hardware
ablations, and concurrency sweeps. Separate fusion and multiplier-bank studies
distinguish primitive gains, complete-request benefit, and physical cost.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\subsection{Two workloads, two communication graphs}
Both standards operate on degree-256 polynomials, but ML-KEM uses seven NTT
layers modulo $q_K=3329$ with 16-bit coefficients, whereas ML-DSA uses eight
layers modulo $q_D=8380417$ with 32-bit coefficients. With lane $l$ holding
$a[l+32k]$, $0\le k<8$, the first three layers exchange registers within one
lane. The remaining layers exchange lane $l$ with $l\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}d$;
$d=16,8,4,2$ for ML-KEM and additionally $d=1$ for ML-DSA. This layout gives
coalesced loads and stores and keeps every intermediate coefficient in GPRs.

Both schemes also call SHA-3/SHAKE~\cite{fips202}. Mapping Keccak word $A[x,y]$ to lane
$x+5y$ exposes the 25-word state, but a round still needs three distinct
patterns: five-lane column parity ($\theta$), a rotate-and-gather permutation
($\rho\pi$), and three-lane row logic ($\chi\iota$). On RV32 every 64-bit word
occupies two GPRs and rotations cross their boundary. A useful instruction
must therefore see both halves while returning only one architectural word.

\subsection{Why the execution boundary matters}
Register residence removes inter-stage memory traffic, but it does not by
itself remove communication instructions. The fair baseline is consequently
a cooperative shuffle kernel with the same state placement, rather than a
single-lane scalar routine. Conversely, fusing more work is not always better:
it shortens the instruction stream but enlarges the unit and can serialize
independent warps. A pointer engine makes the opposite trade, removing a whole
permutation from the ALU path while exposing state transfers through the LSU.
We evaluate all three boundaries under identical request semantics.

This reasoning also applies to the multiplier bank. A 32-lane paired
butterfly needs 16 products, but peak issue rate is useful only while NTT work
is on the request critical path. NTT-PIM and BP-NTT similarly choose their
operation boundary from physical data placement~\cite{nttpim,bpntt}. We apply
that principle to the warp register file and size the arithmetic with
end-to-end measurements. More generally, PacQ and SynGPU show the value of
matching SIMT hardware to irregular dataflow~\cite{pacq,syngpu}; our focus is
fixed cryptographic communication with exact software compatibility.

\subsection{Relation to prior cryptographic accelerators}
Scalar PQC extensions optimize a different register boundary. RISQ-V keeps
polynomial state in wide scalar registers and fuses several Kyber layers,
whereas Nannipieri et al. use twiddle state and packed butterfly outputs to fit
a scalar instruction stream~\cite{risqv,nannipieri}. Those encodings avoid a
SIMT lane exchange but cannot exploit the 128 independent butterflies already
present in every transform level. Our pair instruction instead treats two
lanes as the natural producer of two results while preserving one destination
per lane.

GPU mappings expose more parallelism but usually leave communication in
software. ML-Cube maps Keccak to 25 threads and uses shuffles around
machine-learning accelerators~\cite{mlcube}; the NVIDIA collective-round
description places a full 64-bit round behind a warp instruction
~\cite{collectivepatent}. We retain the 25-lane layout but compare three
implementation boundaries: software shuffles, stage collectives, and a fused
round. This comparison is necessary on RV32, where low/high results double the
architectural operations and a fused unit duplicates internal work to remain
stateless.

Dedicated accelerators can hide these interface costs with private SRAM and
custom schedules. CHAM, NTT-PIM, BP-NTT, and ZK-Flex demonstrate the
performance of co-designed polynomial storage and
compute~\cite{cham,nttpim,bpntt,zkflex}. Their
boundary is appropriate when the accelerator owns the application state. Our
target retains sampling, packing, rejection control, and both standardized
algorithms on one programmable core; the added units therefore operate on the
same GPR state as the surrounding code. We evaluate fixed communication with
software mapping and concurrency held constant, using complete requests.

\subsection{SIMT constraints and design requirements}
Vortex issues one instruction for a warp and carries a thread mask with the
operand vectors. Each lane reads the same architectural register names, while
the result arbiter and scoreboard treat the returned lane vector as one
destination. A collective may use values from several lanes internally, but
it must still retire with those ordinary semantics. Retaining a partial round
or transform inside the unit would instead require a new context to be saved
on a warp switch; writing two architectural destinations would require a new
scoreboard and recovery rule. We therefore require one explicit transaction
to consume source vectors, compute a fixed communication step, and return one
value per active lane.

The active mask must contain every producer needed by that step. For Keccak,
the 25 state lanes execute as one converged group and the unused lanes are
padding. For an NTT at distance $d$, activity must be closed under
$l\mapsto l\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}d$; otherwise one lane of a butterfly would
read an absent partner. Distance, direction, modulus, and Keccak round index
are therefore explicit instruction fields rather than hidden unit state.
These rules are simple enough to assert in simulation and allow a stalled
collective to carry the same warp ID, mask, and destination metadata as the
existing variable-latency units.

The experiments preserve library semantics and inputs. The NTT bank sweep
changes only internal scheduling, keeping the binary fixed. We select hardware
using complete requests, routed core cost, and model parity.

\section{Design and Implementation}
\subsection{Collective contract and core integration}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} places the units beside the integer ALU. An
instruction consumes complete source vectors and the active mask, then returns
one destination vector through the existing result arbiter. No operation
contains a pointer, writes memory, or retains architectural state after
writeback. Keccak requires a converged warp; NTT requires pair-closed masks.
Padding lanes return zero. These constraints permit static wiring and allow
different warps to interleave without saving a cryptographic context.

The decoder uses one custom opcode family and a compact function field for
operation, low/high selection, NTT direction, modulus, and XOR distance. The
scoreboard sees the same source and destination registers as the execution
unit; no implicit register or memory dependence is introduced. A collective
accepts an instruction only when its entire input vector and metadata can be
captured. It holds warp ID, destination, mask, and lane results under output
backpressure, so a stalled result cannot be paired with a later warp. This
property is especially important for a serialized NTT instruction, which
occupies several internal beats but commits exactly once.

\subsection{Keccak: stages, rounds, and RV32 halves}
For coordinates modulo five, the datapath implements
\begin{align}
C[x]&=\bigoplus_y A[x,y],\nonumber\\
T[x,y]&=A[x,y]\oplus C[x{-}1]\oplus\mathrm{rol}(C[x{+}1],1),\nonumber\\
B[y,2x{+}3y]&=\mathrm{rol}(T[x,y],\rho[x,y]),\nonumber\\
A'[x,y]&=B[x,y]\oplus(\neg B[x{+}1,y]\wedge B[x{+}2,y]).
\end{align}
The \emph{Stage} interface exposes $\theta$, $\rho\pi$, and $\chi\iota$.
Each has low/high RV32 forms returning the selected half. Six issues form
a round. The \emph{Round} interface
fuses all stages and uses a literal round index. Its low/high instructions
recompute the internal full word from identical operands, avoiding hidden
state and multi-destination writeback. Two issues form an RV32 round. A
memory-coupled engine that reads and writes the 200-byte state provides the
coarser comparison point.

Stage has two pipeline registers and accepts one operation per cycle. Round
registers parity, $\theta$, $\rho\pi$, and $\chi\iota$ in four steps and also
has initiation interval one. Its larger internal state explains why it can
improve an isolated permutation while producing little request-level gain.
For $\theta$ and $\rho\pi$, both half instructions read both old halves because
rotation carries cross the 32-bit boundary. Software therefore writes the
first result to a temporary until the second issue has consumed the old pair.
$\chi\iota$ reads one half and a warp-uniform round index. Round encodes the
index as an immediate, enabling the 24 rounds to be expanded without a table
load or branch in the permutation body.

\subsection{NTT: one product, two lane results}
For an XOR pair, pair-low lane $p$ supplies $a$ and twiddle $\zeta$; pair-high
lane $h=p\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}d$ supplies $b$. A forward instruction computes
\begin{equation}
t=\Mont(b\zeta),\qquad (r_p,r_h)=(a+t,a-t).
\end{equation}
One product thus serves both lanes, and each lane writes its own destination.
The inverse instruction first forms a sum and difference, then multiplies the
appropriate branch. Lane-local \code{NTTMUL.K/D} handles the first three
layers, inverse scaling, ML-KEM base multiplication, and independent ML-DSA
pointwise products.

A bank of $M$ signed $32\times32$ multipliers serializes the 16 logical pair
products without changing the ISA or binary. CT and ML-DSA inverse
butterflies use $16/M$ beats; ML-KEM inverse and 32-lane scalar products use
$32/M$. The direct latency and initiation interval are $6+B$ and $B$, where
$B$ is the beat count. Operands are captured on the first handshake, lane
results are assembled by index, and backpressure freezes the serializer and
product pipeline. Pair routing, capture, and writeback are shared; only the
constant-arithmetic reductions for $q_K$ and $q_D$ differ.

The shared bank preserves each library's native residue representation rather
than converting coefficients at its boundary. For ML-KEM, operands are
sign-extended from 16 bits; a shift--add Montgomery factor and the constant
20159 Barrett product produce the forward and inverse results. ML-DSA uses the
full signed $32\times32$ product, its Montgomery inverse, and $q_D$ in the
reduction stage. Both modes therefore use the same operand capture,
multipliers, beat controller, and result assembly. Modulus selection changes
only the constant paths after multiplication. This organization lets
\code{NTTMUL.D} serve both butterfly products and ordinary pointwise products
without a second arithmetic unit.

Twiddles remain explicit lane operands. The unit contains neither a twiddle
ROM nor a layer counter, so it does not assume a transform schedule or retain
state between issues. Software can use the same operation for forward and
inverse transforms, final scaling, ML-KEM base multiplication, and ML-DSA
pointwise work. More importantly, the scheduler may suspend a warp between
layers and issue another warp without saving an accelerator context.

The paired encoding addresses a scalar instruction's operand mismatch. A logical
butterfly has three values $(a,b,\zeta)$ and two results, but the pair-low lane
provides the twiddle and the two physical lanes independently receive $r_p$
and $r_h$. This converts an awkward three-input/two-output scalar operation
into an ordinary two-vector/one-vector SIMT transaction. Figure~\ref{fig:detail}
shows M2 assigning successive pairs to two multipliers over eight beats,
and the RV32 temporary that preserves the old Keccak state. Result
assembly records their lane indices and releases the vector only after the
final reduction; later warps can still execute other functional units while
the issuing warp waits on the destination dependency.

\subsection{Complete-library integration}
The native libraries retain their public APIs and byte-level
outputs~\cite{mlkemnative,mldsanative}. Their Keccak and NTT hooks select the
shared datapaths. Lane zero broadcasts primitive arguments, activates the
cooperating lanes, and resumes scalar control when the primitive returns. One-shot SHAKE
keeps its state in registers across absorb, permutation, and squeeze; an
incremental context is stored between calls. NTT kernels load eight
coefficients per lane and perform all layers without intermediate memory.
ML-DSA also reuses \code{NTTMUL.D} for ordinary pointwise products. The
ML-DSA-65 five-term matrix-vector sum accumulates in 64-bit software and
performs one final Montgomery reduction, preserving the library representation.

Cooperation is confined to primitive kernels. All 32 lanes enter and leave a
region together; Keccak activates lanes 0--24, while an NTT butterfly uses a
pair-closed mask at its encoded XOR distance. The leader controls allocation
and rejection loops; warp kernels also parallelize candidate filtering,
encoding, noise generation, and signing arithmetic. This keeps public
interfaces unchanged and prevents a
collective from observing a partially active pair. The compiler intrinsics
name every source and destination, so the existing scoreboard orders a later
consumer exactly as it does for an integer ALU result.

One-shot sponges and transforms keep state in GPRs between initial load and
final store. Pointer Keccak instead transfers the 200-byte state through the
LSU, allowing independent ALU work from another warp.

Full-RAM ML-DSA reserves an 88-KiB arena per request for sets 44/65 and
160 KiB for set 87. Leader-only allocation and reverse-order release reclaim
temporaries after every signing attempt. Simulation
assertions check converged Keccak masks, pair-closed NTT masks, uniform round
indices, backpressure, and warp metadata. Unit tests cover all pair distances
and FIPS-202 rounds. Complete runs of all six parameter sets compare keys,
ciphertexts or signatures, and shared secrets byte for byte with portable C.

\subsection{Model and RTL verification}
The SimX timing model mirrors every RTL bank beat, pipeline latency, and result
stall. This provides a functional oracle before processor RTL runs and makes
cycle disagreement observable rather than hidden by a looser simulator. The
processor tests then exercise instruction decode, scoreboard hazards, masks,
and writeback arbitration. XRT adds the AFU integration used by the complete
applications. We require identical retired instruction counts for matched
SimX/XRT binaries and keep timing gaps below the pre-defined 5\% parity bound;
we do not widen the bound to accommodate a new unit.

Unit testbenches cover all five XOR distances, both directions and moduli,
masks, backpressure, and consecutive warp metadata. Instruction tests compare
every lane with scalar references; transform boundary vectors precede complete
library KATs. All 60 single/eight-request A--E cases across the six sets pass
both models with identical instruction counts and at most
\HeadlineParityMax{}\% cycle difference. Their five board repetitions also
match the XRT instruction counts. Independent post-route cores measure each
hardware choice.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Methodology}
The board experiments use one RV32IM Vortex core with eight 32-thread warps,
16-KiB instruction/data caches and local memory, a write-through D-cache,
no L2/L3, and F/D disabled (Table~\ref{tab:setup}). The shared NTT bank has two
multipliers. Independent cores and the complete AVED image use Vivado~2025.1
OPT3 at 200 MHz; the board clock is read back before and after measurement.
A complete request includes keypair, encapsulation, and decapsulation for KEM,
or keypair, signing, and verification for DSA. We report device-side makespan,
excluding host transfers, as the median of five runs after one warmup.

All arms share warp sampling, KEM encoding/noise/linear arithmetic, and DSA
SHAKE extraction, pointwise accumulation, and signing arithmetic. Zeroization
uses the warp with one worker and the leader otherwise. A uses software shuffles for
Keccak and NTT; B adds Stage, C adds shared NTT, D adds both, and E also uses
\code{NTTMUL} outside transforms. Opcode audits ensure A contains no PQC
instructions and A/B contain no NTT instructions, including Montgomery
conversion. Arms share the same ordered input cohort and board image.
All 156 board cells (780 timed runs) pass byte-exact reference checks.
XRT validates the 60 A--E cases at one/eight requests. Separate bank-sizing
and fusion controls use ML-KEM-768/ML-DSA-65 and retain their archived
write-back cache configuration.

\subsection{Communication collectives improve complete requests}
Figure~\ref{fig:requests} uses a single complete-mapping cohort. At one
request, E accelerates ML-KEM by \KemParameterMinOne{}--\KemParameterMaxOne{}$\times$
and ML-DSA by \DsaParameterMinOne{}--\DsaParameterMaxOne{}$\times$ over A.
At eight requests, the ranges are
\KemParameterMinEight{}--\KemParameterMaxEight{}$\times$ and
\DsaParameterMinEight{}--\DsaParameterMaxEight{}$\times$, respectively.
Accelerated single-request latency is \KemLatencyMin{}--\KemLatencyMax{} ms
for KEM and \DsaLatencyMin{}--\DsaLatencyMax{} ms for DSA.

The ablation separates each hardware contribution. Across parameter sets and
one/eight requests, Stage alone gives \KemStageMin{}--\KemStageMax{}$\times$
for KEM and \DsaStageMin{}--\DsaStageMax{}$\times$ for DSA (A/B).
With Stage fixed, shared NTT adds \KemNttMin{}--\KemNttMax{}$\times$ for KEM
and \DsaNttMin{}--\DsaNttMax{}$\times$ for DSA (B/D). NTT therefore retains
measurable request-level value after Keccak acceleration; these gains do not
establish that it becomes the dominant remaining phase.

Reusing the modular unit outside transforms adds
\DsaArithmeticMin{}--\DsaArithmeticMax{}$\times$ for DSA (D/E), with no new
RTL. KEM's corresponding range is only \KemArithmeticMin{}--\KemArithmeticMax{}$\times$;
set 768 is within 0.2\% of D. Arithmetic reuse is thus useful
but not uniformly profitable. All headline results use E. Within each matched
case, $(A/B)(B/D)(D/E)=A/E$; ratios from other mapping or input cohorts are
not combined with these results.

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} separates occupancy from batch size. On eight fixed
inputs, increasing workers from one to eight raises E throughput by
\ParameterOccupancyMin{}--\ParameterOccupancyMax{}$\times$. With eight workers
fixed, extending batches from eight to 64 changes throughput to only
\ParameterBatchMin{}--\ParameterBatchMax{}$\times$ its eight-request value.
Intermediate DSA batches peak at \ParameterBatchPeak{}$\times$, so throughput
is not monotonic. Batches use synchronized waves. DSA signing retries vary by input;
extending a batch also extends its input prefix, so its curve includes both
amortization and workload variation.

\subsection{Primitive peaks do not select the hardware}
Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoffs}(a) compares Round with equally unrolled Stage.
At one and eight active warps, fusion improves dependent permutation
throughput by \FusionOne{}$\times$ and \FusionEight{}$\times$, respectively.
Its matched complete-KEM gains are only \FusionKemOne{}$\times$ and
\FusionKemEight{}$\times$.
In a separate instrumented control, Stage permutation time is only
\StagePermPct{}\% of a request; sponge I/O, sampling, packing, and control
limit the remaining benefit. This profile is not a phase breakdown of the
six-parameter board cohort. Fixed-mapping XRT controls also find Round
\RoundVsPointerOne{}$\times$ faster than Pointer at one request, but Pointer
\PointerVsRoundEight{}$\times$ faster at eight. Fusion and offload therefore
require request-level evaluation at the intended concurrency.

vectors. SimX and RTL differ by at most \BankParityMax{}\%. The flat request
curve therefore reflects latency hiding and limited remaining NTT work,
not a change in the executed instruction stream.

Stage, Round, and Pointer add \StageLutPct{}\%, \RoundLutPct{}\%, and
\PointerLutPct{}\% LUTs. M2 reduces whole-core LUTs and DSPs by
\BankTwoCoreLutSaved{}\% and \BankTwoCoreDspSaved{}\% relative to M16.
The directly routed M2+Stage core adds \JointStageLutPct{}\% LUTs over M2.
The separate board AFU contains the optional Round and Pointer units as well;
it occupies \BoardAfuLuts{} LUTs, \BoardAfuFfs{} FFs, and \BoardAfuDsps{} DSPs
and closes at 200 MHz with \BoardWns{} ns setup slack. Board runtime ratios
select software paths within this all-feature image, not different core areas.

\subsection{Design implications and scope}
Bank sizing is workload-specific: fewer warps or more transform work could
move the M2 knee. The six-parameter board results validate the selected design;
they do not establish an optimal bank size for every parameter and workload.
Energy requires workload activity; device timings do not measure host-to-host
latency.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}
\section{Conclusion}
Communication-matched collectives keep Keccak and NTT state in ordinary lane
registers and share one modular unit across ML-KEM and ML-DSA. At 200 MHz,
all six parameter sets gain \ParameterMinOne{}--\ParameterMaxOne{}$\times$
for complete single requests over matched cooperative software. Controlled
ablations show incremental NTT benefit after Stage, while bank sizing and
batch sweeps favor modest hardware and explicit warp concurrency. Primitive
throughput alone does not predict the best complete-request design.
